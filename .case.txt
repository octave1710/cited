===== PAGE 3 =====
How we think about this case
Two things matter more than anything else.
We care about your judgement, not your output volume. This case is intentionally larger than you can finish in the 
time we're suggesting. That's on purpose. We want to see what you choose to build first, what you deliberately 
leave out, and whether you can explain why. A smaller, well-reasoned build with clear guardrails will beat a 
sprawling one that does everything shallowly.
We want something that runs, not a plan for something that could run. A working prototype — even a rough one — 
tells us far more than a polished deck of ideas. Mock or stub anything expensive, flaky, or access-gated LLM calls, 
paid APIs, credentials) rather than letting it block you. We're testing engineering judgement, not who's willing to 
spend money on API credits.
You're encouraged to use AI-native tools throughout — Claude Code, Cursor, Google AI Studio, MCPs, whatever you 
actually work with. We'd rather see how you wield them than watch you avoid them. If they helped you move fast, 
tell us how in the walkthrough.
Time: Aim for a focused 68 hours total across both parts. Please don't exceed that — if you run out of time, stop 
and write down what you'd have done next. 
Part A is the tighter, more defined build (roughly a third of your time); Part B is the open-ended one.
===== PAGE 4 =====
Case 1 Background
AEO Page Optimiser
The scenario
A client has a page that ranks perfectly well in 
classic Google search but essentially never shows 
up when someone asks an AI assistant — 
ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, Google's AI 
Overviews — a question it should be the perfect 
answer to. They want to understand why, and 
what to change.
===== PAGE 5 =====
A tool that takes a page and tells the client, concretely, how to make it answer-engine-ready.
At minimum it should:
●
Ingest a page — from a URL (fetch and parse) or pasted HTML.
●
Assess it against the factors that make content citable by answer engines. That's your call to define and defend, but we'd 
expect things like: entity and topic clarity, question-and-answer structure, the presence of clean extractable/quotable 
statements, factual density, schema.org markup, freshness signals, how well the content "chunks," and signals of source 
authority.
●
Produce a score or breakdown a strategist can read at a glance.
●
Output specific, prioritised edits. Not "improve clarity" — actual suggested rewrites, additions, and restructures, ordered by 
expected impact.
●
Generate valid structured data (e.g. FAQPage, Article, HowTo) that the client could drop in and validate.
●
Wrap it in a simple interface — a small web app, a CLI with clean output, a Sheet write-back. The interface layer is part of what 
we're assessing, but keep it thin.
If you have time (you may well not — that's fine):
●
Compare the page against whatever is currently being cited for the target questions, and reason about the gap.
●
Actually test the page: put its content to a live LLM and see whether it gets cited or paraphrased before and after your 
suggested edits.

Case 2 Background
Multi-Market Content Engine
The scenario
The same client wants to scale content across 
several markets — say the UK plus two of the 
Nordics, or any market set you prefer. They've 
heard "just use AI to generate it." A good engineer 
doesn't just say yes to that. We want the workflow 
a strategist operates: reliable, grounded, and 
quality-controlled — not a button that sprays out 
articles.
===== PAGE 7 =====
A pipeline that takes a topic and a set of target markets and produces market-ready, optimised, localised content — passing through a 
quality gate before a human approves it.
A sensible pipeline looks roughly like:
1.
Input — a topic and target markets (optionally a target page or seed keyword).
2.
Per-market grounding — keyword and SERP research per market, from real data or a realistic mock. Crucial point: the 
best-performing term in Swedish is rarely the literal translation of the English one. Ground each market on its own.
3.
Brief generation — a content brief built from that grounding, not from thin air.
4.
Draft generation — the content itself.
5.
Optimise — for classic SEO and for answer engines. This is where you reuse your Part A optimiser.
6.
Localise — cultural and market adaptation, not translation. Consider hreflang and i18n, and the fact that each market's SERP is 
different. Flag clearly where machine localisation needs a human's eyes.
7.
Quality gate — quality scoring, plagiarism / AI-detection checks, and a human-in-the-loop approval step. Decide what must 
never publish without a human.
8.
Output — structured and close-to-publishable, with the metadata a CMS or strategist would need.
This is bigger than the time allows. Build the spine end-to-end before you polish any single node. Tell us what you prioritised and 
why.
One thing we're genuinely looking for: where do you refuse to automate? Mass-generating AI content at volume can hurt a client's 
site. A senior engineer pushes back on the naive version of this request and designs controls instead of just throughput. Show us that 
judgement.

What to hand in?
Both parts.

===== PAGE 9 =====
How we’ll evaluate.
We're a transparent bunch, so here's exactly what we're weighting..
●
Commercial framing — did you connect the build to real client value, or just make a clever tool?
●
Prioritisation — what you chose to build first and cut, and whether you can defend it.
●
Systems thinking — is it architected to run with minimal supervision and be maintained by 
someone else, or is it a one-off script? We'll look at error handling, config, secrets, and the 
README.
●
Grounding and guardrails — especially in Part B: is the output built on real data, and are there 
quality controls and human-in-the-loop gates where they matter?
●
Localisation judgement — adaptation per market vs. literal translation.
●
AI-native fluency — how you used AI tooling to move fast.
●
Communication — could a non-technical client understand your one-pager?
A candidate who builds less but reasons clearly about trade-offs, guardrails, and where a human must 
stay in the loop will score higher than one who ships more with no controls and no rationale.
Good luck — and have fun with it. We're looking forward to seeing how you think.
